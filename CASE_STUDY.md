# Sandbox Tracker — Case Study Log (working title)

*A human-readable record of design decisions, written for a portfolio reader. DESIGN.md is the working spec; this file is the story and the reasoning. Add an entry whenever a real decision is made or changes. Last updated 2026-11-16.*

**Claims-calibration rule (carried from Plot and Cura):** describe the project at the stage it is actually at. Do not present a concept as a shipped product or invent user numbers. Say what was decided, what was tested, and what wasn't.

---

## The problem

Habit and goal trackers lose most people within weeks. Research from the University of Washington on abandoned self-tracking (Epstein et al.) points to the cost of effort, lost interest and tools that assume you'll track forever. Practitioner writing adds three familiar patterns: novelty fades after roughly 3–8 weeks, one missed day ends everything, and a single fixed punishment system suits some people and badly stresses others.

Gamified trackers split users. Habitica is loved by gamers and described by non-gamers as overhead. Minimal trackers like Streaks are loved for speed and clarity but offer little motivation beyond a streak.

## The insight

The gap isn't a better rule set. It's that the designer picks one balance for everyone, and people need different things: some want points and randomness, some want nothing but a checklist, some want to be pushed and some can't stand it. So the product lets each person design the system, and treats the system itself as something worth sharing, the way game players share builds.

## Decisions

**2026-11-16 · Solo sandbox, not a coach tool.** Early framing had a "dungeon master" who sets goals for others. Corrected: the user is the designer of their own system, and friends can be invited into it. *Why:* the tool exists so balancing matches the user's needs, not a designer's or a coach's.

**2026-11-16 · Sharing is free and never monetized.** *Why:* sharing is the growth loop and the content (people reading each other's systems). Charging for it would cut off the thing that makes the product spread. A creator shop with commission is a possible future, not a plan.

**2026-11-16 · Rules and world are separate layers.** *Alternatives:* a single game-style system with points as the base. *Why:* separating them lets "no points" and "lots of points" both be valid choices of the same product.

**2026-11-16 · Guided opt-in onboarding instead of AI.** *Alternatives considered:* an AI that turns a typed description into a ruleset; a blank editor. *What changed our mind:* research shows heavy setup is where people drop off, and a guided "what interests you?" flow ends on a working setup with no cost per user, no unreliable output and no API dependency. AI remains possible later because the ruleset is a defined data format and onboarding is just one way to fill it. Customization depth, not AI, is the hook.

**2026-11-16 · Daily use stays fast whatever the ruleset.** *Why:* the creator's own gym tracker works because it's barebones and quick. Setup can be rich; logging must be one tap.

**2026-11-16 · Plan for lapses.** Lapse behavior (comebacks, pauses, expiring rules) is part of the rule model. *Why:* the research says tracking apps fail when they assume users never stop.

**2026-11-16 · Preview rules before committing.** A simulator shows what a ruleset would do over a bad week. *Why:* people can't judge a rule's effect from reading it, and the creator has already built simulations for a previous game project.

**2026-11-16 · Monetization designed from the start.** *Why:* on Plot it came last; here the paywall can shape the product. Fixed constraints: sharing stays free, editing your own system stays free. Core reason to pay is still open.

**2026-11-16 · Scope not limited by default.** *Why:* the creator's build pace on Plot (about a week for the MVP, about three months of polish) means starting ambitious is low risk.

**2026-11-16 · Mobile app from the start, no website.** *Alternatives considered:* a web app (reaches everyone with a link, but needs separate desktop and mobile design passes), an app plus public web pages for shared setups (growth surface and search presence), an app only. *Why app only:* a daily tracker needs to be one tap from the home screen, the previous project (Plot) was built for the web mainly for search and the creator found that a poor fit for daily use, and sharing can happen inside the app. *Tradeoff accepted:* a friend without the app must install it before they can see a shared setup, and reaching iPhone users costs an Apple developer account and review. Android friends are the first testers.

**2026-11-16 · Sharing by in-app code.** *Alternatives:* a public web page per shared ruleset. *Why:* keeps the product to one surface. A ruleset is one document, so the code can be that document, encoded.

**2026-11-16 · A small tracking core with opt-in modules.** *Alternatives:* one fixed game system with settings to turn parts off; an open-ended rule builder from day one. *Why modules:* a few base trackers (check, count, timer, number with a unit, and a set-list for lifts) cover most things people log, and everything else (points, streaks, health, levels, loot, quests) becomes a module someone opts into. This matches the research that people engage more with game elements they chose themselves (Tondello and Nacke, 2020), and it avoids a blank editor. *Why not assign styles by quiz:* the evidence that matching people to "player types" improves engagement is mixed, so people choose elements directly.

**2026-11-16 · Rules can't trigger other rules.** *Why:* studies of people writing trigger-action rules find loops, conflicts and redundant rules are the common mistakes. Rules run in a fixed order, one pass per event, and only change state. Complex custom rules live behind an advanced editor with a plain-language explanation and the simulator.

**2026-11-16 · Three levels of control.** Pick modules, then adjust dials, then edit rules. *Why:* progressive disclosure, so beginners never see the deepest layer and power users can reach it. Design guidance warns against using customization to avoid making decisions, so each module needs an opinionated default.

**2026-11-16 · Entries are stored apart from rules.** *Why:* history survives rule changes, and a new ruleset can be replayed over past entries to preview its effect.

**2026-11-16 · Sharing revised: friend system plus links, not just codes.** *What changed:* the earlier plan was an in-app code only. With a real friend system, a code is mostly unnecessary. The better experience is a link you can send anywhere that asks for install if needed, then opens the app to add a friend or join their setup. *Tradeoff:* a link needs a small redirect host, so "no website" becomes "no real website," and carrying the shared item through an install is easier on Android than iPhone. A code remains as a fallback.

**2026-11-16 · Barebones tracker first, options added one at a time.** *Alternatives:* build the full module system up front. *Why:* a few options at a time shows which ones people actually want. *Safeguard:* the event system is built from day one, so modules plug in later without reworking the core.

**2026-11-16 · Onboarding shows its work.** Presets for common types of people, plus guided questions, ending on a readable list of every setting the answers produced. *Why:* customization is the hook, so the person should see where their choices landed and be able to change any of it.

**2026-11-16 · Social is opt-in.** *Why:* social features and real-world stakes can raise commitment, but not everyone wants them, and the product's premise is that people choose their own pressure.

**2026-11-16 · Systems over game art; each person gets a unique front.** *Alternatives:* a game world as the main presentation (the Habitica-like direction). *Why:* game visuals turn some people off, which is why Cura moved away from that look toward a badge that showcased a person's focuses. Here the equivalent is a front assembled from the modules a person chose, so it's unique to their setup and worth showing friends. Themes and art stay optional.

**2026-11-16 · Offline-first by default.** *Clarification:* this doesn't mean no backend. It means the app works fully on the device, and the backend and social features are layered on later. *Why:* the creator's own gym tracker works because logging is instant; a network wait in a gym with poor signal would break that.

**2026-11-16 · Stack: Expo with TypeScript.** *Alternatives:* a web app (rejected earlier), native iOS and Android separately. *Why:* one codebase covers both phones, and a browser preview lets the work be checked without a device. Testing on a phone uses Expo Go over the local network.

**2026-11-16 · First build: the barebones tracker.** Three base tracker types (check, count, number with a unit), one-tap logging, undo, and local saving, with an event system in place but no modules yet. *Why:* the core has to feel fast on its own before anything is added to it. Everything the spec describes beyond this is still design, not built.

**2026-11-16 · From one tracker type to many, and what the pitch actually is.** Building the first screen exposed that a gym set (weight and reps, repeated) doesn't fit "check, count, number." That pointed to a bigger idea: not one customizable habit tracker, but a person's whole set of different trackers (lifts, food, weight, habits) under one customizable motivation system. *Competitor check:* the "track anything" builder category is already crowded (Nomie, Perfice, LifeTracker, Trackr, Trackly, Peeved, AnyLog and more), mostly freemium, and some cap free users at one tracker. A tracker builder alone is not a differentiator. *What the search did not find:* streaks, rules and game elements designed once and applied across all of a person's trackers, setups that can be shared, or a unique showcase front. *Decision:* make multi-tracker the foundation, and pitch the system, not the builder. *Design consequences:* a tracker becomes a set of typed fields (with repeating groups for sets); each tracker defines named measures, and rules operate on measures, which is what lets one rule span different trackers; templates keep each tracker as fast as a specialist app. *Risks noted:* a specialist gym app will always out-polish a generic tracker on its own turf, and a builder invites the blank-page problem, so templates matter.

**2026-11-16 · Two levels: trackers feed a system.** *Options on the table:* (a) one customized macro tracker, like the earlier Cura direction; (b) many separately customized trackers in one app, each in its own sealed-off section; (c) a mix where different trackers feed a shared system. *Chosen:* the mix. *Why:* only trackers gives a crowded track-anything builder, and only the macro gives a thin layer with nothing underneath. The value is in how the levels connect: shared state, rules that span trackers, and a front built from all of them. *Terminology change:* "worlds" originally meant a tracker sealed off in its own section. That became the "system" idea: a container with its own trackers, modules and rules. A tracker can join a system or stay standalone, so unrelated things never have to interact. Several systems per person are supported in the data model but not exposed at first.

**2026-11-16 · Onboarding in two passes, with the first log early.** Pick what to track (with presets and explanations), then choose how it's tied together (streaks, game elements, notifications), then see a list of the resulting settings. *Tradeoff discussed:* customization is the pitch, so a longer onboarding is justified, but earlier research shows setup-heavy flows lose people. *Resolution:* only one tracker is required, system questions have defaults and stay reachable later.

**2026-11-16 · Modules use slots; every change is explained.** Same-slot modules are mutually exclusive, different slots stack. *Why a ledger:* in a system the user designs, a score changing without an obvious reason erodes trust. Each change records which rule caused it.

**2026-11-16 · Monetization direction.** Free tier generous enough to feel the full pitch; paid adds more systems, advanced modules and the rule editor, extra field types and themes. *Rejected:* a hard cap on tracker count, which competitors use and users complain about. This is a hypothesis to test, not a settled plan.

**2026-11-16 · A long build session: what got made and what the build changed.** With the direction set, a deep prototype was built in one stretch to have something concrete to react to. It includes eight tracker presets, a system with a forgiving-streak slot plus points, levels, hearts, lucky and all-clear bonuses, simple custom rules, a week simulator, a ledger, a three-step onboarding that ends on a readable list of settings, and a front. *Design changes the build forced:* (1) a gym set became its own entry rather than a repeating group, which kept the data model simpler; (2) the engine replays history instead of reacting to events, so changing a rule re-scores everything and the simulator is the same code with synthetic days; (3) the slot idea was made concrete as one streak style at a time while the game modules stack. *A problem the simulator exposed:* hearts that drop when any tracker is missed punish people more the more trackers they add, so a typical week drained a three-tracker system to zero hearts. This is a good example of why the preview exists, and the likely fix (per-tracker hearts, or a majority rule) is a decision for the next session. *Name check:* "Omni" is crowded; a published research project and an app are both called OmniTrack.

**2026-11-16 · Reading the creator's own gym tracker changed the type model.** The first prototype modeled trackers as subject-named presets (Lifting, Food and macros). Looking at the creator's real tracker showed how much granular control one person wants, and most of it was missing: a weekly plan with rep ranges, checklist foods that carry macros and variants, previous performance shown inline, plan versions with effective dates, rolling averages, first-class rest days, and a plain-text export and import used to revise the plan with an outside AI. *Decision (direction):* types become generic behaviors named for what they do (Sets, Checklist, Measure, Count, Check, Timer), and presets become setups whose supporting text explains what each is good for. *Why:* a subject-named type only serves that subject, while a behavior-named type serves many (a Sets type fits lifting, study blocks and intervals). *Also noticed:* the existing export and import is already a "bring your own AI" loop, which fits the earlier decision to leave AI out of the product and may double as a sharing format.

**2026-11-16 · Trackers become workspaces, and home becomes an overview.** The first prototype treated a tracker as one line on a list. *Correction from the creator:* adding a tracker should be like getting a whole tracker of the kind they built for themselves, opened from a home screen that only gives the overview, with depth and customization living inside each one. *Alternatives considered:* a flat list of one-line trackers (the prototype); one tracker with typed blocks shown inline. *Chosen:* a hub of workspaces, each made of sections (a gym workspace might hold a workout section, a weight section and a meal section). *Consequences:* the home screen needs a small summary contract from every tracker (headline value, progress, streak), which is the same measures idea that feeds the system; presets become templates; every tracker gets the same universal features (history, plan editor with versions, targets, rest days, text export and import). *Test for the model:* if the generic section engine can reproduce the creator's own Fitness Tracker from configuration alone, the model is right.

**2026-11-16 · One universal tracker with a toolbox, and a ceiling on depth.** *Correction from the creator:* nutrition is its own tracker, trackers have no internal sections, and settings do not sit inside the tracker. There are no fixed "types" either: every tracker has the same tools, and the difference is how many are switched on and how many entry fields it has. *Why this beats types:* one engine and one set of behaviors serve a water counter and a full training log alike, and presets become starting configurations with explanatory text. *Use and build are separated:* the daily screen stays fast and the builder holds the depth. *How deep to go (research):* the design target "low floor, high ceiling, wide walls" (Resnick and Myers) says easy to start, powerful enough to grow into, and room for different uses. The "configuration complexity clock" and the inner-platform effect warn that configuration with variables and conditionals slowly becomes a worse programming language. Anecdotes from Notion and Obsidian users describe building elaborate empty systems and abandoning them. *Decision:* the ceiling is a fixed ten-tool box with derived measures from a fixed menu, no formulas, and cross-tracker logic only in the system layer. New trackers start at the simplest setup and depth is opt-in. *Caveat:* the evidence on complexity and abandonment is mostly anecdote, not studies.

**2026-11-16 · Depth is surfaced in tiers, learned partly from other builders.** *What the existing "track anything" builders do (thin evidence):* Trackr offers a template or a custom build and exposes raw options like cron expressions for reminders; Trackly is built around user-defined templates with field types; Perfice adds goals and correlations in a local-first app; LifeTracker lets people define fields, layouts, filters and scoring rules, which suggests a steep learning curve though no source confirmed it. I found no review evidence on how any of them handle complexity, so this is not a verdict on them. *Decision:* each tracker's builder shows a few main switches in plain language, collapses the rest by tool with a one-line summary of its current state, and puts advanced items last; unused tools appear as quiet "Add" tiles. *Why:* the goal is that depth never jumpscares a user who just wants a preset, while people who love customization can keep going.

**2026-11-16 · Units live at the app level, with per-field overrides; data stored in base units.** *Decision by the creator:* a default unit system in settings, changeable per tracker per field. *Design consequence:* values are stored in kilograms, kilometers, centimeters and milliliters and only converted for display and entry, so switching units can never corrupt history. Fixed labels like kcal and grams never convert. *Verified:* switching to metric turned 135 lb x 8 plus 45 lb x 8 into 653.2 kg of volume.

**2026-11-16 · Export and import became three plain-text features.** *Decision by the creator, drawn from their own tracker:* export data so people can hand it to an AI for review, import data, and import structure. *Design:* a readable data export, a machine-readable backup that merges by id, and tracker structure as validated JSON with readable errors. Structure is also the sharing format, which replaces the earlier "code" idea and keeps AI out of the product while still letting people use their own.

**2026-11-16 · The rebuild, and what it showed.** The prototype was rebuilt around the universal tracker: fields, a weekly plan with versions, previous values, targets, rolling averages, units, a builder separate from the daily screen, and a home overview. *The test:* the creator's own gym and nutrition trackers were reproduced from configuration alone and checked by tests; the full plan, set rows, variants, totals against targets and the success rule all work with no special cases. *What the build surfaced:* (1) a sample preset whose items could never reach its own target (95 g of protein against a 120 g target) was only noticed when demo history made every day a miss, which argues for a validation check on presets; (2) a set ticked with no numbers needed a smarter fallback (last time, then the set above, then the default) to feel fast; (3) quick logging works from home for counters and check-ins, but anything deeper needs the tracker screen, which is the intended split. *Not verified:* behavior on a physical phone, and how the builder feels to someone who is not its author.

**2026-11-16 · Course correction: the setup was too complicated, so slow down and restart the setup layer.** After trying the rebuild, the creator's verdict was that it reproduced their gym tracker in full but was confusing to set up and nowhere near as light as intended. *What went wrong:* the builder exposes the engine's vocabulary (fields, measures, targets, derived measures, success rules, unit kinds, slots, variants) and asks the person to assemble a tracker from tools, which is the "configuration complexity" trap this project had researched and said it would avoid. Building wide and deep unattended also meant nobody pressure-tested the setup until it was done. *What stays:* the engine and the proof that the model can express a demanding real tracker. *What changes:* setup should be driven by what a person wants to do, not by which tools to switch on, with depth hidden until asked for. *Lesson:* the model being able to express something is not evidence that people can set it up; test the setup with a concrete bar (how many taps and how long for a simple tracker and for a full plan) before building the screens.

**2026-11-16 · The setup principle: bite-sized steps that show how each answer shapes the thing.** *Starting point:* the first builder put every option in one place. *First proposal:* typed plain-text plans, which the creator rejected because they don't set their own plans up that way and nothing in the product writes them. *What was kept from the correction:* adding a tracker should be an onboarding of its own, covering fields and everything else, not a settings screen. *Alternatives considered for the plan step without AI:* an in-app editor, a template to edit, pasting a structure, and building the plan from a first logged session. *Chosen as the default:* build it as you go, because it turns setup into use. *The wireframe:* six short screens with one question each, defaults always chosen, and a small live preview of the tracker that updates as you answer. *The principle in the creator's words:* break a long menu into bite-sized parts that clearly show how they pertain to the thing someone is trying to set up. *Consequence:* there is no separate builder; editing a tracker reopens the same flow. *Still to test:* whether the live preview helps, and whether the "how you log" and "goal" steps should merge for simple trackers.

**2026-11-16 · Built the guided setup for a counter and training, and measured it.** *Scope kept small on purpose:* after the over-built v3, only the flow was rebuilt and nothing else expanded. *Result against the bar:* a counter took three taps (pick Counter, use defaults for the rest, start logging); a weekly plan with sets reached a usable tracker in three taps too, and the plan builds itself as exercises are added on the day. Editing a tracker reopens the same flow. *What the build decided:* (1) the plan for a day is created by adding the first exercise on that day, with no separate plan screen; (2) a "use defaults for the rest" shortcut on every step turned out to carry most of the lightness, because presets pre-answer every question; (3) onboarding shrank to one question, how pushy the app should be, and then hands off to the same flow used for every tracker. *What was given up:* per-field unit overrides, extra targets and success-rule variants no longer have screens; they live in presets and structure import. *Honest gaps:* the checklist (meals) cannot yet be built as you go, and the live preview and merged steps have only been judged by their author.

**2026-11-16 · Navigation: Home becomes the hub, and "Front" is retired.** *Starting point:* three tabs (Home, Front, System). *Problems the creator named:* the term "Front" was unclear, it was not obvious what that tab did that Home did not, and the System tab mixed app settings, game rules and developer tools. *Alternatives considered:* keep three tabs but rename them; Today, Front and Settings with the rules inside Front; and one hub. *Chosen:* Home is both overview and navigation, with a system summary at the top that opens a Progress page and a guided "edit how it works" flow; Settings behind a gear; no tab bar. *Why:* each place now has one job, there is one fewer concept to learn, and the system's rules are edited with the same bite-sized flow as everything else. *Also decided in direction:* UI customization (theme, accent, density, corners, layout) and a cleanup of the visuals and buttons, with the button problems still to be named.

**2026-11-16 · Built the Home hub, Progress, Settings and a live theme layer.** *What changed:* three tabs became one hub with a system summary, a Progress page, and Settings behind a gear, with no tab bar. The system's rules became a guided flow like adding a tracker, and first-run onboarding became that flow. *The customization decision in practice:* the theme layer is live, so choosing a theme, accent, density, corner style or home layout restyles the whole app at once; Settings itself is the preview. *Technical choice:* the existing code read colors and styles from shared objects, so instead of rewriting every screen to take a theme argument, those objects now read the current theme each time they are used and the root re-renders on a change. *Honest limits:* the button system was left alone because the creator had not yet named what is wrong with it; tracker icons are still emoji; the tracker screen and setup flow picked up theme colors but were not otherwise cleaned up; the accent swatch was not visually confirmed. *Process note:* the browser preview showed stale errors from files being replaced mid-reload, which cleared after a full reload; checking page text and saved settings was more reliable than screenshots.

**2026-11-16 · Feedback round: unified icons, OLED instead of Mono, more hues.** *The creator's reactions:* buttons are fine, the accent works, emoji icons look weak and should be a single library or removed, Mono should not be a theme (a white accent and an OLED mode cover it), and Forest was the only hued theme. *Decisions:* (1) trackers store an icon name from one outline icon set, with a picker in setup and an option to hide icons entirely, which covers both "a unified library" and "none at all"; (2) Mono became OLED, a true-black theme that keeps normal colors, and white became an accent, which reads as ink on light themes so it never disappears; (3) three more hued dark themes (Ocean, Plum, Ember) and a cool light one (Snow). *Why the data model changed:* emoji in saved data and imports would have kept leaking in, so the model stores icon names and maps old emoji on load. *Alternatives considered:* keeping emoji as an option, which was dropped to keep the look consistent. *Honest limit:* the icon picker was typechecked but not exercised in the preview.

### 2026-11-16: Start screen leads with examples

Nick's reaction: the "what do you want to track" options were right, but the useful part was the list of things each is good for, and the long descriptions would not be read. Decided: each starting point shows example chips first (Lifting, Bodyweight, Rehab...) and a one-line descriptor under them. The "pick the closest one" line is gone because the options explain themselves. Principle for the rest of setup: cut instructional text where the choices are self-evident. Alternative considered: keep the paragraph and bold the examples; rejected as still too much reading.

Follow-up, same day: chips are prefixed "e.g." so they read as examples, the one-line descriptor moved under the option name as a subtitle (it earned its spot), "Start from scratch" became a quiet link below the list, and "Paste a structure someone shared" was removed from this screen until the social side is built. Structure import still exists in code and Settings is untouched.

### 2026-11-16: One fixed spacing, two-column start

Nick found the start screen spacing poor and said density and corner settings make it hard to know what to tune for. Decided: remove Density and Corners from Settings and fix one value (soft corners, tighter spacing) while the core experience is tuned; they can return once the design settles. The start screen became a two-column grid of six cards (icon, name, subtitle, three examples), which fits on one phone screen. Cost: less personalization for now, which is deliberate: tuning one look is cheaper than tuning three.

### 2026-11-16: Balancing the start cards, and when to polish

Cards had uneven gaps because text length varied. Fixed by giving name, subtitle and examples fixed-height slots with a divider, bigger icons (34), two examples per card, and "Start from scratch" as a full-width outlined button. Nick also asked whether doing UI polish as we go is wasted effort. Answer given: polish on a screen is worth it when the screen is the one being decided now, because spacing and hierarchy change what the flow means (the example chips only worked once they were laid out well); it is waste when it is global styling that the flow may still reshape. Working rule: structure and content first at the macro level, then one screen at a time to a "done for now" bar, then move on.

### 2026-11-16: Interaction constants, record screen tidy

Decisions: (1) All tapping and scrolling goes through one place, `src/constants/` (press feedback, retention area, double-tap guard, keyboard handling, a feedback() hook for future haptics, sound and animation) and the shared `Tap`, `Button`, `Chip`, `Option` and `Scroll` in `ui/kit`. Considered per-screen tweaks; rejected because haptics and animation would drift. Press now fires on release as before, but shows a pressed state, cancels if the finger slides off, and ignores a second tap within 350ms. (2) On the record screen, "Numbers and notes" offers only the things that fit the chosen starting point (a blank tracker offers all); anything added by hand stays with that tracker only. (3) The unit in brackets was removed from the labels: it was information only, and the unit shows next to the number when logging. (4) Icon set grew from 28 to 100 Ionicons (MIT licensed, free for commercial use). (5) Scroll containers keep focused inputs visible above the keyboard, with extra bottom space.

### 2026-11-16: Keyboard handling and icon categories

The keyboard still covered the add-your-own field on Nick's phone. Cause: relying on the OS to resize the window is not dependable (modern Android draws edge-to-edge and no longer resizes). Decision: the shared `Scroll` listens to the keyboard itself, adds space equal to its height and scrolls the focused field above it, with the margins and timing in `constants/interaction.ts`, so every screen behaves the same on both platforms. Alternative: a keyboard library; kept as a fallback if this proves flaky on devices. Also: the start screen scrolled because the footer padding applied to every step; now only steps with the fixed footer get it. The icon picker is grouped into ten categories (Move, Food, Health, Mind, Create, Time, Life, Money, People, Stats).

### 2026-11-16: Name step, clearer choices, no skip button

Decisions: (1) Naming got its own step right after the starting point: the name starts empty with an example as placeholder (a preset name like "Counter" described the type, not the thing tracked) and is required; the icon stays preset and the category picker is always open there. (2) Choice cards were reworked so the headline carries the meaning: larger bold title, a smaller one-line subhead, an icon, and a radio mark. The cards used to shift when selected because the border changed from 1px to 2px; the border is now always 2px. The log question now reads "Whenever I do it" and "Follow a weekly plan" with an example in each subhead. (3) "Use defaults for the rest" was removed from both flows: the flow is short, and reading each screen is how someone learns what the defaults are. Cost: no way to skip ahead for experienced users; revisit if people find it long.

### 2026-11-16: Icon search, preview card removed

Decisions: (1) The icon picker gained a search box with hand-written keywords per icon (gym, money, sleep and so on), as phone emoji pickers have; the category tabs stay for browsing and are hidden while searching. Keywords are never complete, so the tabs are the fallback. Rejected: search over icon names alone, since names like `fitness-outline` do not match what people type. (2) The dashed preview card at the top of every setup screen was removed: it was oversimplified, repeated the summary step and cost vertical space. The idea it served, showing how an answer shapes the tracker, is better done as a short line under a choice at the point of decision (not built yet).

### 2026-11-16: Icon coverage pass

Nick asked for an icon-by-icon check so search finds what people mean. Done: the set grew from 100 to about 190 Ionicons across eleven categories (added Digital, plus sports, food, health, home, money and people icons that were missing), and every icon now carries a list of plain words people might type. Search forgives plurals and endings (lifting, smoke/smoking, running/run) and requires every typed word to match. A test file lists about 250 common searches (gym, swim, anxiety, dishes, quit smoking, budget and so on) and fails if any finds nothing, so gaps surface as the keyword list changes. Honest limits: the library has no swimmer, boxing glove or climbing icon, so those searches land on the nearest icon (water, fitness); the keyword lists are my judgment, not user data, so real searches will find more gaps. Alternative considered: a second icon library for gaps; rejected for now to keep one visual style.

### 2026-11-16: Picker opens on the likely category

The icon picker now opens on the category that fits the starting point (Weekly plan on Move, Checklist on Food, Measurement on Health, Time blocks and Check-in on Time, Counter on Stats) and still shows every category. It is a nudge that can save a tap and show what the options look like, not a restriction. Editing an existing tracker opens on the category of its current icon.

### 2026-11-16: Keyboard and icon search results

Nick noticed that the keyboard hides the icons while searching: keeping the field visible is not enough when the results sit below it. The shared `Scroll` now supports `focusToTop`, used while the icon search field is focused: it scrolls the field near the top so the results show in the space above the keyboard. ## 2026-11-17: Correction on "offline-first", and an opening screen for name and look

**Correction.** Earlier entries and DESIGN.md said "offline-first". That was a misreading of "solo first". The product will have a backend (Supabase is the likely choice) for accounts, cross-device sync and the social side. The constraint that stays is that logging must feel instant, so an entry is recorded without waiting on the network. Nothing is designed around staying offline. Consequence: a name is a reasonable requirement at the start, because accounts will need it.

**Decision: the first screen asks for a name and the look.**
- *Why open with it:* it gives a real sense of control over the experience from the first minute, and it is the first taste of the product's main idea (you shape it). It is one short screen with defaults that already work.
- *Options considered for colour:* (a) keep the fixed list of accents; (b) add a free colour picker only; (c) presets for speed plus a proper picker for freedom. Chose (c), because most people stop at a preset and the picker costs nothing for them.
- *Two colours, not one:* the app already had a background (theme) and a highlight (accent). Both stay. Each is offered with presets and a picker, and each suggests partners for the other that keep enough contrast, so a free choice is guided and not blind.
- *Picker style:* three sliders (colour, richness, brightness) plus a hex box, over a 2D square or wheel. Sliders are easier to hit with a thumb on a small screen and the tracks show the result before you let go.
- *Contrast:* one background colour is turned into the full set of surfaces and text. Text is made as readable as the background allows. A highlight that would disappear is nudged to a 3:1 contrast, not blocked. Tests check this across ten backgrounds including awkward mid-tones.
- *Name required:* chosen because accounts will need it; easy to relax.
- *Still open:* what the default look should be for people who never customise. Currently Auto (follows the phone) with a blue highlight.

## 2026-11-17: Text size follows the phone; research on how background and highlight should relate

**Text size, decided.** Defer to the system font size instead of adding our own control. Research agrees: React Native text follows the phone's setting by default, and turning scaling off (or fighting it) is considered bad practice because people depend on their device setting. The work is making layouts survive large sizes (text wraps, nothing fixed-height clips), and at most capping a few cramped elements such as the bottom bar labels. To be tested at 130% and 200%.

**Research on the colour system (sources are vendor and community articles, so treat as convention, not proof).**
- Material 3 turns one seed colour into palettes. Backgrounds and surfaces come from a *neutral* palette, only lightly tinted by the seed, not from a separate choice. The user picks one colour and light or dark.
- Theme-picker guides: follow the phone's light/dark by default with a manual override; offer roughly 5 to 8 accent presets; use previews; check accent contrast.

**Finding about our own picker.** The suggested backgrounds do change with the highlight (checked: deep tint and opposite shift hue per highlight), but all are near-dark and the page itself does not change until one is picked, so it feels disconnected. Also fixed: a white or grey highlight had no hue and produced a red tint; it now uses a calm blue-grey.

**Proposal under discussion (not built):** replace the nine theme tiles with Mode (Auto, Light, Dark) plus Highlight, and derive the background from them (neutral, or tinted by the highlight, with pure black and "your own" as options). Fewer choices, and changing the highlight visibly retints the app.

## 2026-11-17: Background as a mode that follows the highlight (built)

Nick's reading of the proposal: choosing Deep tint for a green highlight and then changing the highlight left the background green and no longer on Deep tint. The suggestions were stored as fixed colours, so they could not follow. Decision, in his words and ours:
- Highlight first. The background is a *mode* shaped by the highlight: Light, Dark, Deep tint, Opposite. Light and Dark are plain neutrals, and the phone's setting picks which is selected by default (so "Auto" is gone as a separate option). Forest, Ocean, Plum, Ember and the rest are dropped, since each was a deep tint of one hue.
- A mode that follows the highlight is stored as a kind and recomputed from the current highlight, so the selection holds and the hue changes with it.
- Manually choosing a colour ("Your own") is the escape hatch: it is fixed, and later highlight changes no longer move it.
- Open: Deep tint and Opposite are dark, so a person on light mode has no tinted option. A light counterpart (soft tint) is cheap to add if wanted.

Tests added: each highlight gives its own tint, tints stay dark and keep the highlight visible, and a white or grey highlight no longer tints red. 75 tests pass. Not tried by hand on a phone.

## 2026-11-17: Schedules for every tracker, not just plans (proposal, not built)

Nick's point: "in order" versus "set days" is a gym framing. Any tracker can have its own rhythm: every day, every other day, certain weekdays, one week only, the last day of each month. Scheduling should be a property of every tracker, shown cleanly, with the choice guided by what the person needs rather than by mechanics.
- *Model:* a tracker has a schedule of one of these kinds: every day; certain weekdays; every N days from a date; a number of times a week or month on any days; once a month (a day, or the last day); or whenever (no schedule). Separately, an optional window (start, end) for "just for a while". Today only plans know about days, and everything else is implicitly daily.
- *Why it matters beyond display:* "due today" has to feed the success rule, streaks, the tick in the bottom bar and which tracker the app opens on. A check-in that is due every other day must not count the off days as misses.
- *Plans:* set weekdays keep a list per weekday (unchanged). Every N days rotates the sessions by occurrence from the start date. A number of times a week on any days runs in order (next up, found from what was logged). This replaces the calendar-cycle idea, which needed a Day 1 date and rest-day marking.
- *Choosing:* one question, "How often does this come up?", with plain-sentence options and examples; the summary shows one sentence ("Mon, Wed, Fri"), with the weekday toggles inline for the common case.
- *Open:* a "3 times a week" schedule overlaps the existing weekly "show up" goal, so one should imply the other instead of showing two similar items.

## 2026-11-17: Schedules, stage 1 built (model and rules; no screen yet)

Agreed with Nick: every tracker has a schedule, chosen from plain options, with sensible defaults per starting point. This step builds the model underneath; the Days card, the Days screen and the wording pass are the next steps.
- *Model:* a tracker's schedule is one of: every day, certain weekdays, every N days from a date, N times a week or month on any days, once a month (a date or the last day), or whenever. An optional window (start, end) limits it to a span. All the date rules live in one file (`core/schedule.ts`) and the rest of the app asks it one question, "is this day expected". Day 31 in a short month means the last day; the every-N-days count does not drift across clock changes.
- *Where it bites:* the existing "expected today" check now consults the schedule, so a day the schedule skips is neither a miss nor a success. The app opens on the first tracker that is due and unfinished (a rest day or a "whenever" tracker is never waiting on you), and the week strip shows skipped days as rest.
- *Defaults:* Gym split and Full body on Mon, Wed and Fri; Running three times a week; Spending, Symptoms and Savings whenever; the rest every day.
- *Times a week or month:* it becomes one real weekly (or monthly) goal of that many days, stored once, so it is never listed twice and survives editing without touching goals the person added themselves.
- *Existing trackers:* saved before schedules existed, they keep behaving exactly as before (plan trackers train their plan's weekdays, everything else is every day), derived on the fly with no data migration.
- *Not yet:* the screens to see and change it; plans that rotate by occurrence (every few days) or run in order (times a week); the wording of the summary lines.
- *Mistake on the way:* a code edit with a dollar sign in the replacement text silently duplicated parts of the templates file. It was caught at once by the type-check and the tests and repaired; noted because the symptom (a mass of failing tests) looked far from the cause.
87 tests pass (11 new, covering due days, month ends, a clock change, sentences, the window, old trackers, defaults and the weekly goal). Not seen on a phone.

## 2026-11-17: Plan and days consolidated: the schedule is the one place days live

Nick asked whether the split between plan and days had been dealt with. It had not: stage 1 added a schedule but left the old weekday list beside it, so "which days" was stored in three places (the setup answers, the schedule, the plan's rest marks) and edited in three places (a row on the summary, chips on the plan screen, a Rest day switch in the plan editor).
- *Decision:* the schedule is the only answer to "when". The plan is only the lists (what you do on a day) and what each day is called. Rest marks in the plan are derived from the schedule, never chosen separately.
- *Built:* the setup answers lose their own list of days; the Days card on the summary is the single place to choose them, for every tracker (plans included); the plan screen's day chips are gone (it says what the days are and points to the summary, and still names each day); the plan editor's Rest day switch now changes the schedule and the plan follows it.
- *The Days card:* a dropdown that explains each kind in a line, with the controls for the chosen kind under it: seven weekday toggles, an every-N-days stepper with a start (today, tomorrow, next Monday), a times-per-week-or-month stepper, a day-of-month stepper with a last-day switch. ## 2026-11-17: Quests: goals that belong to the system

Nick clarified what "macro" meant: generalised quests that span trackers, such as completing three tasks from different trackers, kept in the system, applying to chosen trackers, with their own spot. My earlier reading (the daily "good day" rule) was wrong; the "A good day" and "Goals" sections on a tracker's goals screen stay, since they were still a fair separation, and quests are the new layer above them.
- *Model:* a quest has trackers (chosen ones, or all), a metric, a period and an amount, stored with the system. Metrics are built from what a tracker already says about a day, so a quest never needs its own logging: trackers completed (each tracker that had a good day counts once for that day), perfect days (every chosen tracker that was due had a good day) and entries logged. Periods are today, each week, each month, or in total from the day it begins, optionally by a date. Always "at least".
- *Why these three:* they cover the example (three trackers in a day), the existing "all trackers hit" idea as something you can aim for repeatedly (five perfect days this week), and plain activity (twenty entries this month). Cross-tracker totals of a number such as minutes were left out: they only make sense when the trackers share a unit, and that deserves its own thought.
- *Points:* meeting a quest over a finished week or month, or by a date, earns points like a tracker's own goals do (a day quest earns none, since a good day already does). The first part-week is not judged. A test checks that a met weekly quest raises lifetime points.

Add a quest opens a builder in the same compact style as the goal maker: a pinned sentence, tracker chips (All, or pick some), Count, Over, At least, and By when for an in-total quest.
- *Not yet:* quests do not show on the tracker pages; there is no way to start a quest from a template; and a quest that counts a number across trackers is not available.

## 2026-11-21: Making the smart opening hard to fool

Worries: odd days diluting the usual time, and the opening reinforcing itself (the app opens a tracker, you log it because it is there, so its usual time drifts to when you open the app). Reasoning: a wrong pick costs one tap on the bar, so the harm is bounded; the aim was that it is never worse than simply reopening on the last tracker. Changes: an abandoned tracker no longer wins by bar order (nothing logged in 14 days sorts it last); weekends and weekdays learn separately; hand-logged past days (stamped noon) are ignored; a quick reopen skips a finished tracker when others are waiting. To test the loop rather than argue about it, a simulation runs 60 days for six kinds of routine, feeding each day's logs into the next day's pick, and checks the pick matches what the person wanted and is never below "where I was". Results: 100% for a regular day, weekend shifts, an unused first tracker and night-time batch logging, 89% with a sick day each week, 87% on a week in another time zone, against 0% for "where I was" because each routine moves between trackers.

Tried and dropped: staying on the last tracker when nothing is near its usual time. It looked kinder but made the simulated week away worse (87% to 79%), and the simulation does not model repeated visits to one tracker, so there was no evidence for it.

Known limit: someone who returns to one tracker many times a day (like water) and a few hours apart gets the usual-time pick, not the last one. If that proves common, the answer is a per-tracker signal, not a global bias.

**Follow-up, same day: is a simpler rule enough?** The same 60-day simulation was run for "first unfinished tracker on the bar" (with the timer, the 30-minute memory and the unused-tracker rule) against the learned-time rule. With a bar in day order and nothing skipped, both scored 100%. The simple rule was slightly better on a weekly sick day (100% vs 89%) and a week away (91% vs 87%). It fell behind when you skip something: 60% when the morning tracker is skipped half the days (the skipped one stays first in line all day), and 33% to 40% when the bar order does not match the day. Decision: keep the learned time. The simple rule is more predictable, but skipped trackers and a bar in a different order are ordinary, and it handles them badly. The routines are my own constructions, so real use is the real test; "Where I was" stays one tap away in Settings.

Reviewing the preset against habit-only apps (Streaks, Loop, HabitNow, Habitify), the single-tap check-in was fine to log but had two problems: you had to name it before anything, and one tracker held one habit, so ticking five habits meant switching trackers five times. The missing parts of a habit app (reminders, streaks and completion rates, goals, rewards, charts) are the shared systems planned for all trackers, so they are not built into this preset. The same goes for skip days and notes, which only make sense once those systems say how streaks count.

Decision: the preset is now a list you tick, the same shape as Medication and Vitamins (items ticked once a day, the same list every day), named "Daily habits" with the item called "Habit", and it starts empty. A single habit is a list of one. Each tick is stored against its own item, so the later streak and chart systems can give every habit its own numbers. The Check-in shape stays as the base for avoid trackers, Bills and Plants, where one tap per tracker is right.

Process: the remaining presets get reviewed in batches of three, pre-screened by Claude (setup clarity, missing toggles, fit against dedicated apps) and then checked again by Nick.

## 2026-11-22: Daily habits wording, and phone steps move into Tools

Two wording fixes on the list shape. The summary's "You log" row said "The same list" while the Days card already said so; it now says what is ticked ("Tick off each habit", "Tick off each vitamin"), and a list that differs per day or is chosen each time keeps its old wording. An empty list that repeats every day said "Add your first habit to Monday", which suggested one day only; it now says "Add your first habit", and names a day only when the lists differ by day.

Connected data now lives where the other tool switches do. When a tracker has a number called Steps, Tools shows "Steps from my phone" (on for the Steps preset) and the summary's Tools row reads "Steps from phone". It is saved as the tracker's source for that field, so it is the same setting as the switch on the tracker screen, which stays for quick changes and for the allow-access prompt. Only steps has a phone source so far; heart rate and the other readings get the same row when they are built.

## 2026-11-22: Preset audit paused; the hub comes first

The preset-by-preset audit is paused after Daily habits, because nearly every preset question (a counter's daily minimum or maximum, a weekly total, days a week) is really a question about goals, and goals depend on how the rest of the app works. Building the shared systems first saves going round every preset twice.

Direction (a theory to refine, nothing built): one overview place, the hub, shows progress across all trackers and is where people customise which trackers appear, how the data is drawn, and over what period. It is meant to be shared: a person's hub should show their style as well as their progress. Goals and gamification are one system that grows out of it: a goal is a measure over a period compared to a target, and streaks, points and levels are what the engine works out when goals are met. Trackers keep showing only the part that concerns them, and the hub is where it all lives.

## 2026-11-22: Example people for testing the hub

Before building the hub and its charts, Developer settings can load six example people, each with their trackers and a realistic history: just started (2 trackers, 5 days), a few for a few months (4, 75 days), regular (7, 300 days), tracks everything (18, 540 days), many for one month (24, 28 days), and few for years (3, 3 years). The point is to see whether the hub looks interesting for every kind of person, not only for someone with a full history. Histories are generated, not random noise: a keen first few weeks, quieter weekends, good and bad weeks, one longer lapse in a long history, readings that drift, lifts that climb and level off, and a usual time of day for each tracker. The same person is built every time. A second testing aid, "Keep trackers in step with presets", rebuilds trackers made from a template whenever the app opens, so template changes show on trackers that already exist.

A finding from building them: the 18-tracker, 540-day person is about 1.6 MB of saved data, and Android's storage can fail on values around 2 MB. Long histories need the move to a real database (expo-sqlite) before real people reach that size, which moves that item up the list.

## 2026-11-22: Supabase set up; sign-in without passwords

Trackbench gets its own Supabase organization and project (a free project was not available in the existing organization, which already held two). Decision: the app stores values in Supabase and syncs to it, local first, so logging never waits on a network. The phone keeps the full data in its own database and Supabase receives changes in the background; every row has a stable id, an updated time and a deleted marker, and row-level security means each person can only reach their own rows.

Sign-in has no passwords. Everyone starts anonymous (no sign-up screen, one tap to start logging). After they have something worth keeping, the app offers to keep it: a Google account, or a six-digit code sent by email for people without one, attached to the same anonymous user so nothing is copied. Sign in with Apple joins when the iOS version does, because the App Store requires it once another sign-in is offered. The weakness of anonymous accounts is that data is lost with the phone until an identity is attached, so the offer has to come early. Passwords were left out because they add a screen and a reset flow and make the app hold a credential to protect.

Addendum, same day: accounts are needed eventually, so data survives a reinstall and so a web version can use the same data. That does not require passwords: a Google account or an emailed code restores the same user on a new phone or in a browser, and email-and-password can be added later to the same user table if people ask for it. Google sign-in waits until after the first sync build, which is tested with anonymous users alone. Open item: the prompt to attach an account must exist before anyone outside the testing group uses the app, because until then their data lives only on their phone.

## 2026-11-22: Saving moves to a real database, and sync to the cloud is built

Everything the app knows used to be saved as one JSON value after every change. The biggest example person (18 trackers, 540 days) made that about 1.6 MB, close to where Android's storage fails, and each tap rewrote all of it. Saving now goes to the phone's own SQLite database: trackers, entries and a few whole values (settings) are rows, and a tap writes one row (about 3 ms for the largest person, which loads in about 110 ms). The app still holds everything in memory and screens are unchanged; the layer underneath works out what changed and writes only that. Data in the old single value is copied across once on first launch; if the database cannot open, the old way of saving is the fallback.

The local tables mirror the cloud ones (stable ids, an updated time, a deleted time, and a flag for "not sent yet"), so sync is an add-on: the phone sends flagged rows, then asks for anything changed in the cloud since it last asked, and where both sides changed the same row the later change wins. Deletions are marked, not removed, so they travel too. Cloud backup is off until it is switched on (Settings > Developer for now), because the data is personal and should not leave the phone without a choice. Sign-in is anonymous. The rules are tested with a stand-in cloud (a second phone receives everything, long histories page across correctly, deletes propagate, a pending local change is not overwritten by an older one).

Known limits, on purpose: the first sync downloads again what it just sent (simple, wasteful once); two phones that change the same entry in the same moment keep whichever the server saw last; an entry's day depends on the phone's timezone; motivation data and the trash are local only.

## 2026-11-22: Accounts are required, with Google first; names and handles kept apart; changes are sent when the app is left

Revised: not opt-in. Either everyone has an account (and so cloud) or there are no accounts; a halfway state (some people local-only, some backed up) was rejected as two products to build and explain. Everyone signs in at the start: Google first (one tap on Android, and it replaces typing a name), with an emailed six-digit code as the fallback. Typing the code proves the address, so there is no separate confirmation email, and sessions last long enough that a code is only asked for on a new phone or browser. The cost is a gate before first use, and the first sign-in needs a connection (after that the app works fully offline, because the phone's database stays the source of truth). Account deletion in the app is needed, and Sign in with Apple joins with iOS. The anonymous sign-in stays for tests only.

Names: everyone has an editable display name, prefilled from the Google profile or the start of the email, on the name screen that already exists. A unique handle is not asked for at sign-up; it is asked for the first time someone does something social (shares a hub, follows someone), together with a choice of what others see, because a Google name is often a real full name and should not become public by default. Handles are lowercase and unique and can be changed only occasionally so links keep working. A small profiles table (display name, handle) joins the cloud schema when social features are built.

Freshness: an entry is on the phone's database within milliseconds, which is the part that must not fail. The cloud copy waits about four seconds after the last change so a burst of taps is one send, and it is now sent immediately when the app goes to the background, because the app may not run again soon. Still to do: a periodic background send through the phone's scheduler as a safety net, and a fetch every minute or so while the app is open (only matters once two devices are used). Live push (Realtime) waits for features that need two people to see each other's changes at once.

Revised again, same day: sign-in is Google only (Sign in with Apple joins with iOS, which the App Store requires once Google is offered). No passwords and no emailed codes, so no email sender is needed. A password cannot be recovered without email, and a code adds a screen, so Google alone is the fastest sign-in and the least to maintain. The cost is that on Android someone who refuses a Google account cannot sign up until another method is added; Supabase attaches several sign-in methods to one user, so email can be added later without touching anyone's data. Named apps that do only this were not found; social-first sign-up is common and Apple's own guidance presents it as skipping forms and passwords.

## 2026-11-22: Required accounts built: Google or email and password, a username from the start, gate first

Decision (replacing "Google only"): the account is required and comes first, and it follows the pattern already used in Plot: a username that is always there, then Google or an email and password. Google only was dropped because email and password gives people the familiar thing and some will not want a Google account; the cost is a password reset email, which needs an email sender (Resend) before launch. Until then the Supabase project can have email confirmation switched off so email and password works without any email going out.

Where the account sits: it is the very first screen, before the name and look screen, because tracking should be backed up from the first entry even if the person never uses anything social. The pitch is one line, "Back up your data": an account keeps everything safe if you change or lose your phone and brings it back wherever you sign in. Nothing else is asked there. The existing name screen then asks for a username too (only when there is none yet), prefilled from the name and checked for being free as you type; the name itself is prefilled from Google. Someone signing in on a new phone gets their name, username and settings back from the account and skips both screens.

Rules that follow from it: backing up is on whenever signed in and everything already on the phone is sent the first time; signing out sends what is waiting and then clears the phone (so the next person to sign in does not receive it), refusing if the last changes could not be sent; deleting the account removes the cloud data and the phone's data (an app-store requirement); only while testing can the sign-in be skipped, and then nothing is backed up. A username is 3 to 20 lowercase letters, numbers or underscores, unique whatever its capitals, and kept apart from the display name, so a real name from Google is not shown to others by default. Still to come: the password reset email, Sign in with Apple (with iOS), and what others may see of a profile (with the social features).

## 2026-11-22: No usernames: your name is your name

Revised the same day the account screens were built: there is one field, your name, and no unique username. Two fields on one screen felt strange and unnecessary, and a name people choose for themselves reads as more personal and leads to fewer odd handles. Names can repeat, because every account has its own unique key; anything that links people (friends, sharing a hub, comparing goals) will use codes or links, not names. Google often carries a name and it is prefilled; when it does not, the name is asked. Removed with it: the username field and its free-or-taken check, the reserved words, and the cloud's usernames table (a small cleanup, "part 3" in the schema file, removes the table made by the earlier version). Cost, accepted: nobody can be found by searching a name, and two people can share a name, so any list of people shows a name with a code to tell them apart, which suits sharing by code.

## 2026-11-22: The hub spec is written, and checked against the six example people

Part 3 of the schema is confirmed applied (no profiles or usernames tables, no handle_available); only settings, trackers and entries remain in the cloud.

The hub is specified in `docs/hub-spec.md`. Decisions: a hub panel is only a source, a shape and a period, so there is one idea to learn; a new hub is built from what data exists (never from what a tracker is for), so nobody meets a blank page; goals, streaks, points and levels are all derived by replaying entries, so edits, imports and new goals give right totals at once and a goal can be tried on the past before it is saved; streaks alone are the default gamification and points and levels are offered; trackers get only a read-only strip and a one-line result; comparison is Spearman rank correlation on shared days, needs 20 days, and says "tends to", never "causes"; sharing is an image card (with a hide-values option) and an importable layout that binds by role, not tracker id.

The paper check against the six people found two gaps, both now rules: any panel over 8 rows shows its top 6 (the 18-tracker person), and a period longer than the history clamps to it and says so (the 28-day person). The 5-day person's hub is thin but honest. Open for Nick: the tab's name, the streak default, where comparison starts, and the image card's look.

## 2026-11-22: Research on goals, gamification, notifications and habit science

Two rounds of searches (full notes and sources in `docs/research-habits-goals.md`), done before building the goals engine so the options people get are grounded. What held up: one missed day barely matters but many do, and forming a habit takes weeks to months with huge variation (Lally 2010); specific goals beat vague ones, and process goals tend to help more than outcome goals; gamification gives small, inconsistent benefits and no study isolated streaks; tangible expected rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation, informational feedback does not; a notification lifts opening in the next hour but fixed schedules wear off; accountability works better with some stake. What did not: there is little rigorous evidence about streaks themselves, and most app-side material is blog posts or company claims. Several well-known claims (a specific meta-analysis on if-then planning, Strava, Habitica, Fogg) could not be confirmed and are listed as gaps.

Design consequences (proposed, for Nick's review): participation is a menu, not one system; streaks get a choice of rule (strict daily, count per week or month, grace, pause) with best streak beside current; goals default to process; rewards lean on informational feedback and points stay optional; notifications use cues and back off when ignored; social starts cooperative; starting points are named by what a person wants (just look, build a habit gently, hit a target, stay accountable) and are bundles of the same settings.
